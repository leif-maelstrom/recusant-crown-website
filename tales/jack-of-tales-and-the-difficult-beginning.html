Jack of Tales and the Difficult Beginning

Fairy Tale

I. Jack and the Beanstalk

Once there was a boy named Jack who lived with his mother up a little hollow at the bottom of a valley, and at the top of the valley, above the clouds, there lived a giant.

Nobody in the valley had ever seen the giant. Everybody knew him. Every morning, as soon as the roosters had finished, he leaned out over the edge of his cloud and reminded them.

"FEE! FI! FO! FUM! I SMELL THE BLOOD OF THE LITTLE ONES! BE THEY ALIVE OR BE THEY DEAD, I'LL GRIND THEIR BONES TO MAKE MY BREAD!"

He had never ground anybody's bones. He had never come down to fetch any. He had never needed to.

The shutters rattled when he shouted. The milk went sour in the pail. The swallows had quit nesting in the eaves, and nobody in the valley had baked a decent loaf in years, because it is hard to knead dough with that line rolling around in your head. Mothers kept their children inside the fence. Men said grace quietly, so as not to be overheard.

And in the evenings, when there was nothing else to do, people sat on their porches and told each other about him. Old Mrs. Pell said he was as tall as the church. Her grandson said he was as tall as the hill. By harvest the whole valley agreed he was taller than the hill, and his voice came down so hard the next morning that it knocked the weathervane off the mill.

Jack was afraid of the giant the way he was afraid of thunder, which is to say he liked it. When the shouting started he would run out into the yard in his nightshirt to hear it better, and his mother would haul him back in by the collar.

"Jack," she would say, "just once in your born days, finish one thing all the way through."

Jack never could. He was the kind of boy who could not walk past an open gate, or a closed one either. He started fences and left them. He started chores and wandered off after a snake doctor. He had a dozen friends in the valley and he would help any of them with anything, for about an hour.

The spring their cow went dry, Jack's mother sent him down to the county seat on market day to sell her.

"Bring back coins," she said. "Round ones. Not a story."

Jack walked the cow down the road, and at the crossroads he met a woman in a gray hood sitting on a milestone. Her hands were very clean, the way hands look when they have never touched anything by accident.

"That's a fine cow," she said.

"She's gone dry," said Jack, because he couldn't help it.

The woman smiled at that, as if he'd passed something. She opened her palm. Five beans lay in it, speckled like a thrush's egg.

"The cow for these," she said.

Jack looked at the beans. "Are they magic?"

"They are beans," said the woman. "They will do what beans do."

That was the most interesting answer Jack had heard in his life. He handed over the rope.

His mother did not think it was interesting. She threw the beans out the window into the dark and sent Jack to bed without a bite of supper. What she didn't know was that Jack had only given her four. The fifth was in his pocket, because Jack never spent all of anything. You never knew.

In the morning the window was green.

Jack pressed his face to the glass and saw leaves, each as broad as a door, and a stalk as thick as the old oak by the mill, going up and up past the roof, past the hill, into the clouds, and on the other side of the clouds, the giant was already shouting.

"FEE! FI! FO! FUM!"

Jack's mother stood in the yard in her apron with her neck bent all the way back.

"Jack," she said, without looking down. "Don't you dare."

But Jack was already ten feet up.

The stalk was cold and wet with dew, and the sap made his palms tacky so they stuck at every grip. The first hundred feet were easy. After that the wind found him, and the valley began to shrink underneath, his mother's house to a thumbnail, the church to a white pin, the river to a thread somebody had dropped. His arms burned. His knees shook. He laughed out loud the whole way, because he had never in his life been anywhere so high, and he couldn't think of one good reason to stop.

Then his head pushed up through the clouds.

The cloud held his weight like a wet mattress. And across it, so big that Jack had to turn his whole head to take it in, stood a castle.

The door was open. Jack walked in under it without ducking, and the top of the doorway was so far above him that a hawk was nesting in it. He crossed a floor of flagstones as wide as his mother's garden. He came to a table and walked between its legs like walking between trees.

And at the table sat the giant.

He was eating porridge.

He was so big that his spoon was a rowboat, and his breath, when he blew on it, made Jack's hair stand up. His face was red and his beard had crumbs in it. Between bites, he got up, went to the window, and shouted down through the clouds at the valley, then came back and sat down and ate some more. He did not seem to enjoy the porridge. He seemed to enjoy the shouting.

There were sacks of gold piled in the corner, and a hen in a cage that blinked at Jack like it had seen things. Jack hardly looked at them. Because when the giant had finished his bowl, he pushed it away and called out:

"HARP! SING ME!"

On the table stood a golden harp, with a woman's face carved into the top of its neck. Her eyes opened. Her strings began to play by themselves. And she sang:

Who is the tallest under the sky?
Who is the loudest, who is so high?
Who do the little ones fear in their beds?
Who is the greatest? The Giant! The Giant!

It was not a good song. The giant mouthed every word. He leaned back in his chair with his eyes half closed and his hands folded on his belly, and as the harp sang he seemed to settle, the way a cat settles in a sunny window, until the chair creaked under him and his head nodded and he began to snore.

Jack stood under the table and looked up at the harp.

He did not want the gold. He had no use for the hen. What he wanted, standing there with the snoring going over him like weather, was to know what would happen if he took the one thing the giant loved best.

He climbed the table leg. He tiptoed across the tabletop past the porridge bowl. He put both arms around the harp and lifted.

The harp hiccuped. Her carved eyes swiveled down to look at him. Her strings shivered. And then, as clear and sweet as a bell, she sang:

Jack, Jack, nimble and quick,
Climbed to the clouds on a green bean stick!
Who is the cleverest, who is so bold?
Jack! Jack! Jack!

The snoring stopped.

"THAT," said the giant, "IS MY SONG."

Jack ran.

He ran across the table and dropped off the edge and hit the floor rolling, with the harp still singing about him in his arms. He ran between the table legs and under the door and out across the cloud, and behind him the giant came thundering, and the castle shook, and the hen in her cage started to scream.

Jack reached the top of the beanstalk and swung himself over.

He went down a great deal faster than he had gone up. He slid where he could and dropped where he couldn't, and the leaves slapped his face and the harp sang the whole way, and above him the stalk lurched and groaned, because for the first time in all his years, the giant had followed something down off his cloud.

Jack looked up.

The giant's hand closed on the stalk above him, and it covered three leaves at once.

A little lower, it covered two.

The giant's shout came down after Jack, "FEE! FI!" and it was loud, but not as loud as it had been in the castle. When they passed the hilltop it was a big man's bellow. When they passed the church steeple, the giant was about as tall as the steeple, and he was puffing, and his beard was full of leaves.

Jack dropped the last ten feet into his mother's turnip patch and stood up.

A moment later the giant landed beside him.

He was a head taller than the blacksmith. Maybe a head and a half. His shirt hung off him like a sail with no wind in it, and his trousers had slid down so that he had to hold them up with one hand. His face was very red. He was breathing like a bellows.

The whole valley had come out to see the beanstalk, and now the whole valley stood in the lane and looked at him.

Nobody said anything.

The giant drew himself up. He puffed out his chest. He opened his mouth.

"FEE..."

His voice cracked on it, high and thin, like a boy's.

Somewhere at the back of the crowd, a child laughed.

Then somebody else did. And then the baker, who had not baked a good loaf in eleven years, put his hands on his hips and said, "Grind their bones with what? You ain't even got a mill," and the whole lane went up in laughter like dry straw.

And the giant got smaller.

Not all at once. Just a little, with every laugh, the way a snowman goes in March. By the time the laughing died down he was no taller than the blacksmith, and he had to hold his trousers up with both hands.

That was when the laughing stopped being funny.

"Eleven years," said someone, quietly.

"I ain't slept a whole night through since my wedding," said Mrs. Pell.

"My boy's never been past the fence."

"My milk."

"My bread."

They closed in around him. The giant looked at Jack, of all people, with his mouth open, as though Jack might know what to say.

Jack didn't. He had planned the climb and he had planned the theft and he had planned the run, and he hadn't planned one single thing past the turnip patch. He shifted the harp in his arms. His stomach felt like it had stayed at the top of the beanstalk.

"He never actually did anything," Jack said.

"He said he would," said the blacksmith.

Jack tried a joke. It was a good one, about porridge. Nobody laughed.

They tried the giant that afternoon out in front of the church, and it did not take long. He was charged with shouting, with boasting, with threatening, with souring the milk, and with ruining the bread of an entire valley for eleven years. He shouted his defense, but it didn't carry. Halfway through, he stopped shouting and just talked, and then nobody could hear him at all.

They hanged him from the oak by the mill at sundown.

Before it was done, the men had sent a boy running to the coast to fetch a ship's cable, because nobody believed an ordinary rope would hold him. The boy hadn't made it past the crossroads before they called him back. An ordinary rope did fine.

And all the while, in Jack's arms, the harp sang.

Jack! Jack! Nimble and quick!

People clapped him on the back. Mrs. Pell kissed him on both cheeks. The blacksmith lifted him onto his shoulders, and from up there Jack could see over every head in the valley, which he had never wanted to do before and found he did not like. Somebody brought out a fiddle. Somebody else brought out a jug. And they told it, all night long, on every porch: how the giant had been taller than the hill, how Jack had fought him hand to hand on the beanstalk a mile up in the air, how the giant had begged.

The giant had not begged. Jack opened his mouth to say so, and then the fiddle started again, and he closed it.

By midnight they were saying the giant had been taller than the mountains.

It was late when Jack finally walked home.

He went in at his mother's front door the way he had gone in ten thousand times, and he cracked his forehead on the lintel so hard he saw stars.

His mother looked up from the fire. She looked at him for a long time, the way she had looked at the beanstalk that morning. Then she got up, took the paring knife off the table, stood him against the doorframe, and cut a mark level with the top of his head.

It was a good two inches above the last one. The last one had been cut at Christmas.

She didn't say anything. She went back to the fire.

Jack wrapped the harp in a blanket and put it in the corner. He could still hear it. He wrapped it in a second blanket and put it in the root cellar and shut the door. He could still hear it, faint and sweet, coming up through the floorboards.

Jack! Jack! Jack!

He lay in bed and listened to it, and through the open window he listened to the valley, porch to porch, telling itself about him. He put his hand in his pocket and found the fifth bean and held it.

And in the morning, his feet hung off the end of the bed.

II. Jack and the Wickergiant

Jack left the valley on a Tuesday, before he could outgrow the house.

He took a loaf, a wedge of cheese, his father's old knife, and the fifth bean in his pocket. He left the harp in the root cellar under three blankets. He could hear it singing until he was over the first hill, and then he couldn't, and then he was almost sure he still could.

On the third day he fell in with a peddler going the same way, a man with a cart full of pots that clanked at every rut. The peddler talked the way the pots clanked, steady and without stopping, and on the fourth day he told Jack a story.

"Down in a valley back east," he said, "there's a boy called Jack who killed a giant. Climbed a beanstalk a mile high and wrestled him hand to hand in the clouds. Ten foot tall, this Jack. Eats a whole hog at a sitting."

Jack hunched lower on the cart seat until his knees were up around his ears. His shirt pulled tight across his back. He could feel it pull a little tighter.

"You ever hear that one?" said the peddler.

"Once," said Jack.

At the fork the peddler went south, and Jack went north, because north had fewer people in it.

You could smell Wickerwood before you could see it: cut willow and green sap and old smoke. It sat in a crook of the river where the withies grew thick as hair, and everything in it was woven. The fences were woven. The roofs were thatched. Baskets hung from every porch beam, and every door in the town was painted the same green, like a row of pole beans.

And above the roofs, at the edge of the square, stood the giant.

He was made of wicker, as tall as three barns stacked on one another. His ribs were bent willow, his chest was a basket big enough to keep a cow in, and his head was a great woven bulb with two knotholes for eyes. Faded ribbons hung all over him, hundreds of them, red and yellow and blue gone gray with weather. He did not move while Jack watched. He didn't need to. Everybody who crossed the square bobbed their head at him as they passed, the way a tall man ducks a low beam, without thinking about it at all.

Jack stood in the road with his mouth open. Then he laughed out loud, because it was the strangest and most wonderful thing he had ever seen.

Nobody laughed with him. A woman crossing the square pulled her little boy behind her skirts.

The innkeeper explained it over a bowl of stew.

Every Midsummer, for longer than anybody could remember, Wickerwood had woven a giant out of willow, stuffed his chest with straw, danced around him all night, and burned him at dawn for luck. And eleven summers ago, the night before the burning, the giant had stood up.

"Stood up?" said Jack.

"Stood up," said the innkeeper. "And he's never sat down since. And nobody's lit him since, neither." He said that last part the way a mother tells a child not to spit in the well.

"Does he walk?"

"Most nights."

"On his own?"

The innkeeper looked at him as though he'd asked whether bread baked itself. "The ropes walk him. Twelve to a leg and eight to an arm. Crews go in after dark and haul."

"What if nobody hauled?"

"Then he'd walk without us," said the innkeeper, "and that'd go a sight worse." He took Jack's bowl. "Crew eats free. You've got the shoulders for it. What do they call you?"

Jack opened his mouth to say his name, and what came out instead was, "Tom."

It came out easy, like dropping a pebble down a well. He listened for the splash, and there wasn't one.

He hauled on the left leg.

The left leg's crew boss was Abel Tuck, who was older than anybody Jack had ever met and had hands like two knots of locust root. Abel had woven the Midsummer giant every year for forty years. He had burned it for twenty-nine of them, and for the last eleven he had not. His granddaughter Nell hauled on the rope in front of Jack's. She had quick hands and a sharp chin, and the first night Jack made a joke inside the giant's knee, she laughed so hard she lost the count.

It was the first time anybody had laughed at one of Jack's jokes since the turnip patch.

Inside the leg was dark and close and smelled of sap and sweat. The ropes were slick with years of hand grease. Abel called the time: heave, and step. Heave, and step. When the leg lifted, the whole basket of the giant lurched, and Jack felt it in his stomach, the same drop he'd felt on the beanstalk. When it came down, the ground shook and the crew all leaned together like barley in the wind.

Nobody inside the leg looked at Jack. Nobody knew his name, because he hadn't given it. He was a back and two hands on a rope, one of twelve, and he loved it so much he could have cried.

Inside a week, his sleeves came down past his knuckles again.

"You're wearing your big brother's shirt," said Nell.

Jack laughed harder than the joke deserved.

There were rules in Wickerwood. Doors were green. You bowed to Old Wicker in the square. And when he walked at night, he went where he went. The crews felt the pull in the ropes and followed it. Nobody steered. Everybody hauled.

One Saturday, Farrier Dunn married a girl from downriver who liked red, and to please her he painted his door red.

That night the ropes pulled toward Dunn's lane.

Jack hauled. They all hauled. Heave, and step. Through the knothole in the giant's knee, Jack watched the red door come up close in the moonlight, close enough to see the brush marks, and then the foot came down.

There was a sound like a hayrick falling over. Splinters and thatch went up in a cloud. Dunn and his bride stood in the lane in their nightclothes, and she was holding his arm, and neither of them was hurt. Nobody was hurt.

In the morning the whole town turned out to help them rebuild. They painted the new door green. Dunn's bride didn't come out to help paint. By the end of the week she had gone back downriver.

Jack looked at his palms that evening. They were blistered from the rope. He had hauled that leg. He had been one back in twelve, which was exactly what he had wanted to be, and it turned out one in twelve was still one.

"Why doesn't anybody burn him?" Jack asked.

Abel was splicing a rope by the fire. He didn't look up. "He's ours."

"He stepped on Dunn's house."

"Dunn painted his door red."

"That's not a crime."

"Here it is." Abel pulled the splice tight with his teeth. Then he did look up, and his eyes were not cruel. They were tired, and kind, and old. "Boy. Every place has got a giant. Ours is made out of us. You don't like it," and he nodded at the window, at the dark road north, "that's the road, right there."

Jack went to bed. He lay awake.

He should have taken the road. He knew it the way a person knows a step is missing in the dark, just before his foot comes down on nothing. But he couldn't stop seeing the red door come up in the knothole. He couldn't stop feeling the drop in his stomach. And somewhere down under the knowing there was a hot, tight, certain feeling that somebody had to do something, and nobody here would, and he had done this before.

In the morning he went to work, and he started paying attention.

He learned which ropes ran to which joints. He learned which ribs were green and which had been drying for eleven years. He learned that every Midsummer, though nobody had lit the giant since he stood up, the crews still climbed up inside his chest and packed it full of fresh straw for luck, the way they always had. He learned where the hatch was in the hip. He learned it all with his hands on the rope and his mouth shut, and nobody noticed, because nobody looked at Tom.

The night before Midsummer, he bought a flint and steel from the chandler.

Midsummer in Wickerwood was fiddles and lanterns and new ribbons. The whole town danced around Old Wicker in the square, and the crews stood inside him holding him steady, because he liked to stand for the dancing. Jack slipped out of the left leg and climbed up through the hip, into the dark basket of the chest, into the straw. It was dry and sweet and prickled his neck. Through the weave of the ribs he could see the lanterns spinning.

He filled his lungs and shouted as loud as he had ever shouted in his life.

"FIRE! FIRE AT THE BAKERY!"

The fiddles screeched and stopped. People ran. The crews came pouring out of the legs and arms like bees from a kicked hive and went pelting down the lane toward the bakery, Nell in front, her skirts in her fists.

All but one.

Through the ribs, Jack could see down into the left leg, and Abel Tuck was still standing there on his rope with his feet set, looking up.

"Abel!" Jack hissed. "Get out!"

"There's no fire at the bakery," said Abel.

Jack's throat closed up. "There's going to be one here."

The old man nodded slowly, as if Jack had told him it might rain. He wrapped the rope once more around his fist.

"Then I'll hold him while he goes," he said.

This was the place for Jack to stop. He knew it. His hand knew it too, and shook, so that the first strike of the steel missed the flint and skinned his knuckle.

The second strike didn't miss.

Eleven years of dry willow went up like a held breath let go. The fire ran up the ribs faster than a squirrel up a tree. Jack dropped out through the hip with his eyebrows singed and hit the cobbles and rolled, and when he looked up, the whole chest of the giant was a lantern.

And Old Wicker moved.

He lifted his left leg. One crew, one old man, hauling alone. The burning giant took one step forward across the square, toward nothing at all, and Jack heard Abel's voice inside the leg, steady, calling the time to nobody: heave, and step.

Then the rope burned through.

The leg came down crooked. The great woven body folded over on itself with a sound like a forest sighing, and Old Wicker sat down in the square of Wickerwood for the first time in eleven years, and burned.

Abel didn't come out.

The town came running back from the bakery, which was not on fire. They stopped at the edge of the square. Nobody said anything. The fire said everything, popping and roaring, throwing sparks up into the Midsummer dark.

Nell stood at the front. She was looking at the left leg.

Then, from somewhere at the back of the crowd, came a voice Jack knew, a voice like a cart full of pots.

"That's him!" said the peddler, who had come north for the festival. "That's Jack! The giant-killer from the valley!"

Every head in Wickerwood turned.

"His name's Tom," said Nell, but her voice wobbled.

"His name's Jack," said the peddler.

Nell looked at him. Jack would have given anything for her to say either name, even the true one, even in anger. She didn't say anything. She turned back to the fire, where her grandfather was.

Somebody pulled a burning withy out of the blaze. Then somebody else did.

Jack ran.

He ran down the lane past the green doors with the torches coming behind him, and the light of the torches threw his shadow out ahead of him on the road, long, longer, enormous, so that he was running after it and could never catch it and could never get away from it either. He looked back once. He was taller than every man chasing him. His sleeves were halfway up his arms.

At the edge of town they stopped. They didn't chase him any further. They just stood at the town line and shouted his name after him into the dark.

"JACK!"

And every time they shouted it, he felt it in his bones, a deep dull ache like growing pains.

He ran until he couldn't hear them. He ran until the moon went down and took his shadow with it. Then he lay in a ditch with his hands over his face and his knees pulled up, and he was too long for the ditch, and he hated it, he hated his long legs and his long shadow and his long name, and he thought: this is how it starts. This is how you get to be a giant.

He put his hand in his pocket and held the last bean until morning.

He didn't plant it. He wasn't sure anymore what would grow.

III. Jack and the Last Bean

Jack went home, because a boy who has done a terrible thing wants his mother, even when he is too big for her door.

The news had gotten there first. Peddlers walk faster than guilty boys.

By the time Jack came over the last hill, the whole valley knew that Jack had gone north and burned a giant made of a hundred men. They knew that a town full of wicker witches had worshipped it. They knew that Jack had climbed into its burning heart with a torch in each hand and fought his way out through the flames with the witches screaming after him. They told it on every porch. They told it to him, when he came down the lane, and children hung off his arms, and the blacksmith wanted to know how many witches, exactly.

"There was an old man in it," Jack said.

"Course there was," said the blacksmith. "Witch-man." And he told it that way that very night, and by the next morning the old man had been a sorcerer with a beard of live snakes.

Every time somebody told it, Jack grew.

He could not get in at his mother's front door anymore, not even sideways. He slept in the barn, in the empty stall where the cow used to be, with his feet sticking out into the yard. His mother brought his supper out to him on a tin plate, soup beans and cornbread. She sat on an upturned bucket while he ate, and she watched him the way she had watched the beanstalk.

"Was there an old man?" she said.

"Yes," said Jack.

She nodded. She didn't tell anybody. In the morning she came out with breakfast and sat on the bucket again and didn't say anything at all, and that was worse than anything the blacksmith said.

Down in the root cellar, under its three blankets, the harp had learned a new verse.

Jack, Jack, bright as a torch,
Burned down the giant and swept up the porch!

One night Jack went down and got it. He carried it out to the chopping block behind the barn and raised it over his head, high, as high as he could reach, which was very high now.

His arms wouldn't bring it down.

He stood there with it over his head until his shoulders shook, and the harp sang about him the whole time, sweet and clear. Then he carried it back down to the cellar, and put the blankets back over it, and climbed the cellar steps bent nearly double.

That was the night Jack started planning.

He lay in the barn with his feet in the yard and his eyes open, and for the first time in his whole life he planned a thing all the way through, from the first step to the last, the way a man crosses a river on a rope: every foot set before the next one lifts. He didn't hurry. He didn't skip ahead to the fun part. There was no fun part.

He went walking along the ridge in the daytime, and people waved to him from the fields, and he waved back and counted his steps. Above Devil's Throat, where the river ran so fast and deep that nothing it took ever came up again, he found a place where the edge was crumbly and a big man might slip. He learned which day the peddler came along the ridge road. Thursdays. He learned to walk up the creek bed on the stones so he left no tracks, and practiced it at night until he could do it without a splash.

On Wednesday night he took off his boots. He walked up the ridge in his bare feet. He scuffed the crumbly edge with his heels, the way a falling man would, and set the boots there side by side. He tore his coat on a thornbush and left the torn piece on the thorns. Then he took his father's knife and drew it across the heel of his own hand, quick, and it stung like a wasp, and he let a few drops fall on the stones at the edge.

Then he went up the creek on the stones, quiet as a heron, and hid in the woods above the churchyard.

On Thursday the peddler found the boots. Jack could hear him shouting from a mile off.

They buried him on Sunday.

There was no body, because nothing Devil's Throat takes ever comes up. So they buried his coat, folded, in a box the carpenter built. They buried it by the church, and people came from three valleys over, more people than Jack had ever seen in one place, and the women laid out dinner on the grounds on sawhorse tables, and they stood in rows and passed the story among them.

The blacksmith said Jack had been taller than the steeple at the end.

Mrs. Pell said he had been the bravest boy who ever lived.

Somebody went down into the root cellar and brought up the harp and set her on the grave, and she sang. It was a new song, slow and lovely, and people wept to hear it. She sang about the beanstalk and the porridge and the turnip patch, and about Wickerwood, and the witches, and the torches. She sang that there would never be another Jack. She sang his name over and over until it seemed to hang in the air above the churchyard like smoke.

Jack's mother stood at the front in her black dress with her hands folded, and she did not cry. Not once. People whispered that the shock had taken her.

Up in the woods, under an oak, Jack held onto the tree so hard that the bark printed itself into his palms. His knees were shaking. Every part of him wanted to run down the hill and push through the crowd and put his arms around her and say I'm here, I'm here, it was a trick, it was only a trick.

He didn't.

Of all the things he had done, the beanstalk and the harp and the hanging and the fire, this was the worst one, and he knew it while he was doing it, and he did it all the way through.

When the crowd went home, his mother walked home alone. She stopped in her own front doorway and stood there a long while. She ran her thumb down the marks on the doorframe, one by one, from the highest at the top to the lowest one down by her knee. Then she went inside and shut the door.

That night Jack went to the crossroads.

The woman in the gray hood was sitting on the milestone, just as if she had been sitting there since spring. Her hands were folded in her lap. They were still very clean.

"I need it to go somewhere," Jack said. He held out the last bean. "Not up. Somewhere else."

"It will go where beans go," said the woman.

"Where's that?"

"Wherever there's room."

Jack knelt in the dirt of the crossroads and dug a hole with his fingers. Before he could drop the bean in, the woman spoke again.

"There is a price."

"I paid you a cow."

"For the beans," said the woman. "Not for the leaving."

Jack sat back on his heels. "What do you want?"

"Your name."

Jack laughed, and it came out cracked. "Everybody has my name. The whole valley. Three valleys. A whole town up north that shouts it in the dark."

"Everybody has a copy," said the woman. "I would like the one you are holding."

Jack thought about the porches. He thought about the harp on the grave and the blacksmith with his hands cupped around his mouth and Nell not saying it, and the men at the town line saying it over and over until it ached in his bones. He thought about how heavy it had gotten. He hadn't noticed how heavy, until now.

He leaned in close to the gray hood, and he gave it to her, quietly, the way a person tells a secret.

It was like setting down a sack of stones he'd been carrying since the turnip patch. He nearly fell over.

The woman folded it up small and tucked it away in her sleeve, as neatly as a coin.

When the boy looked up again, the milestone was empty.

The boy dropped the bean into the hole and covered it over with dirt and sat down beside it to wait.

He didn't wait long.

It came up green and fast and quiet in the moonlight, up past his head, up past the signpost, and then it did a thing the first beanstalk had never done. It bent. It went up, and then sideways, and then somewhere there wasn't a word for, somewhere that made the boy's eyes water to look at, like looking at the sun through a window that isn't there.

He put his hands on it. It was cold and wet with dew, and the sap made his palms tacky so they stuck at every grip.

He climbed.

With every leaf, his sleeves came down a little further. By the time the crossroads was a thumbnail beneath him, his shirt hung loose on his shoulders like a borrowed one. His bare feet looked small on the great green leaves. He didn't know where he was going. He didn't know who he would be when he got there. Nobody did. Nobody at all.

Halfway up, without meaning to, the boy laughed out loud, for the first time since Wickerwood.

Then the fog came in under him, and the valley was gone.

In the churchyard, after midnight, the harp was still singing on the grave.

There was nobody to hear her. She sang anyway. She sang about the beanstalk and the porridge and the fire, and the name, over and over, Jack, Jack, Jack, until the name hung in the air above the grave so thick it almost seemed a hand could reach up and take hold of it.

The moon came out from behind a cloud and laid the headstone's shadow long across the grass.

And the shadow stood up.

It stretched, slow and easy, the way a man stretches when he wakes from a long sleep. It was a good deal taller than the boy had ever been. It bent down and read the name carved on the stone, running a dark thumb along the letters one by one.

Then it smiled, because it had decided the name fit.

And the harp, glad to have somebody to sing for at last, sang louder.
