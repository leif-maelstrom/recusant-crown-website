Puss in Boots

Fairy Tale
Art coming soon

There once was a miller who died and left his three sons a mill, a donkey, and a cat. The eldest took the mill. The middle son took the donkey. The youngest got the cat, and everyone agreed this was the cruelest inheritance of the three, the cat included.

The youngest son sat on the mill step with his elbows on his knees and his chin sinking toward them. He was a pleasant young man. He had a pleasant face and a pleasant slouch, and nobody had ever looked at him twice.

The cat looked at him twice. He circled the young man slowly, tail high, studying the jaw, the shoulders, the hands that had never held anything heavier than a flour sack. A smooth, unmarked surface, waiting.

"Get me a pair of boots and a sack," said the cat, "and I will make you something."

The young man blinked. "Make me what?"

"Admired."

The boots cost the young man his last coin. The cat refused the first three pairs the cobbler offered, turned his nose up at the brown ones, and finally chose red leather with a turned cuff, because red was the color people remembered. He stood in them before the cobbler's mirror for a very long time, turning his hip one way and then the other.

Every morning Puss went to the woods with his sack and came back with a rabbit, and every afternoon he carried the rabbit to the palace and bowed before the King.

"A gift," he said, "from my master, the Marquis of Carabas."

There was no Marquis of Carabas. Puss had invented the name on the palace steps that first morning, because it sounded like something worth being.

The King was a heavy, bored man who liked rabbit. Puss watched him the way a painter watches light. When the King laughed at the herald's joke, Puss became witty. When the King grunted at the bishop's sermon, Puss became humble. When the King leaned toward a story about a boar hunt, Puss became, by the following week, a cat of great sporting reputation. He bent himself into whatever shape the room admired, and the room admired him, and the room admired his master even more, because a cat so splendid must belong to someone splendider.

One day Puss heard that the King would drive along the river with his daughter. He ran home with his boots slapping the road.

"Go bathe in the river," he told the young man. "Right there. Don't ask questions."

The young man waded in, shivering. Puss stuffed his ragged clothes under a rock. When the royal carriage rounded the bend, Puss leapt into the road waving both paws.

"Help! Thieves! They've stolen the Marquis of Carabas's clothes and left him to drown!"

The King sent a footman galloping back to the palace, and within the hour the miller's son stood on the riverbank in blue velvet and a lace collar, water still dripping from his hair.

Puss's breath caught in his chest. The jaw was the same jaw. The shoulders were the same shoulders. But now the light fell on them properly.

The Princess leaned out of the carriage window and stared.

The young man did not know what to say to a princess, so Puss rode beneath the carriage and coached him through the floorboards with his tail: up meant smile, down meant be silent and look thoughtful, and a slow flick meant mention the hunting. The young man mostly looked thoughtful. The Princess found him deep.

When the carriage stopped to water the horses, the young man crouched by the wheel where Puss sat, and tugged at the lace collar as if it were choking him.

"Puss," he whispered. "Who am I supposed to be?"

"The Marquis of Carabas."

"But who is that?"

Puss licked a paw and smoothed his whiskers. "Whoever you like. You could be the Duke of Carabas, if you'd rather. The King of Carabas, someday. It's all in the presentation."

The young man looked at his own hands for a long while. Then the King called, and he climbed back into the carriage and arranged his face into something thoughtful.

Puss ran ahead. In every field he passed, he stopped before the reapers and set his boots wide.

"When the King asks whose fields these are, you'll say they belong to the Marquis of Carabas. If you don't, I'll have you chopped fine as pie filling."

The reapers looked at the cat in red boots and decided they would rather not find out what pie he meant.

All that afternoon the King leaned out the carriage window and asked whose fields these were, and all afternoon the answer came back: the Marquis of Carabas. The King looked at the young man with new respect. The young man looked at his knees.

The fields all belonged, in truth, to an Ogre, who lived in a castle at the end of the road. Puss had heard the stories. The Ogre could become anything.

He found the Ogre alone in a great hall built for a hundred guests. The Ogre was a lion when Puss walked in. Then an elephant. Then a crowned king, then a bishop, then a towering heap of gold, each shape rising and collapsing like a sigh, performing for a hall with no one in it.

Puss's tail went very still.

The heap of gold noticed him. It slumped, and became an Ogre again: enormous, gray, and blushing to the tips of his ears.

"A guest," said the Ogre. He wiped his hands on his shirt, then wiped them again. "You'll forgive me, I wasn't expecting anyone. No one comes anymore. They used to come from three kingdoms to watch me. Kings. A cardinal, once." His eyes slid to the empty chairs. "Was the lion any good? Be honest. I think the mane's gotten worse. I used to do a better mane."

"The mane was splendid," said Puss.

The Ogre's whole body loosened with relief, then tightened again. "You're only saying that. The elephant, though. The elephant's still good, isn't it? Tell me the elephant's still good."

"The elephant was very good."

"I practice every day." The Ogre lowered himself onto the edge of the long table, and it groaned under him. "In case someone comes. You have to be ready, in case someone comes. Do you know what I am when no one's watching?"

Puss said nothing.

"Neither do I," said the Ogre, and laughed too loudly at it, and glanced at Puss to see if it had been funny.

Puss knew this creature. He knew the long table with no one at it. He knew the way the Ogre's eyes went to the doorway after every word, hoping someone had seen.

"Anyone can be large," Puss said softly. "It must take far more skill to become something small. A mouse, for instance. I suppose even you couldn't manage a mouse."

The Ogre's face lit like a lamp. He could. He did, immediately, and sat on the flagstones twitching his whiskers, waiting for applause.

Puss hesitated for the length of one heartbeat. Then he ate him.

He told himself it tasted like nothing at all.

When the King's carriage rolled into the courtyard, Puss stood at the gate in his red boots.

"Welcome," he said, "to the castle of the Marquis of Carabas."

The wedding was the grandest in a generation. Everyone praised the Marquis's lands, the Marquis's modesty, the Marquis's thoughtful silences. The Princess held his arm and glowed. The King slapped his back.

No one praised the cat.

The Marquis gave Puss a velvet cushion by the fire, a silver dish, and fresh fish every morning for the rest of his life. Puss curled up on the cushion and closed his eyes.

His tail twitched. He rolled over. He rolled back. He lay with his eyes shut, listening to the castle, and every voice in it was a voice that might be talking about someone else.

On the third morning a traveling painter stopped at the gate, looking for a commission. Puss was off the cushion before the man finished his sentence. By the end of the month, a portrait of the Marquis on a white horse hung in the great hall, twice life size, and visitors came from the next valley to see it.

Then a neighboring lord began grumbling over a border stream. Puss arranged a parley, a feast, and an unfortunate accident involving the neighboring lord's boots and the stream. The King was so impressed with how the matter was settled that he made the Marquis a Duke.

Then the bards needed a new song. Then the bridge at the river needed a new name. Then the Duke of Carabas needed a statue, and a holiday, and a second portrait to answer the first.

With each one the Duke's collar grew heavier, and his chain of office longer, and he stood up a little straighter under the weight of them, and sat down a little more slowly at night.

At last one evening he found Puss in the stables, polishing his boots by lantern light.

"You've given me everything," said the Duke. He knelt in the straw, the way he used to sit on the mill step. "I've been trying to think what to give you back. And it came to me. I never named you. I'd like to give you a name. A good one."

Puss's ears went flat. He backed up a step, the way a cat backs from cold water.

He had taken the boots and the sack. That was the bargain, and he had paid it back a thousand times over, in fields and castles and portraits and bridges. A name was something else. A name handed over for nothing, he could not hold. It would sit on him like borrowed clothes.

"No," he said. "Thank you. No."

The Duke's face fell. "Then what will you do?"

Puss stood. He stretched until his spine cracked, and he shook out his whiskers.

"I'll make one."

He pulled on his boots, left and then right. On the road that morning, a tinker had mentioned a goose girl two valleys over: plain, quiet, and unnoticed by everyone.

The Duke of Carabas watched him go from the castle steps. The Cat in Boots did not look back, though he did check his reflection in the first puddle he passed.
