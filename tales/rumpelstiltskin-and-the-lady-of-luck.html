Rumpelstiltskin and the Lady of Luck

Fairy Tale

There was once a weaver who owned a loom, a daughter named Mara, and a mouth that ran faster than his shuttle.

One midsummer the king held a fair on the green below his castle. There were ribbons and pies and a pig in a hat. Among the stalls walked a lady in a dress the color of new leaves, wearing a crown of dandelions gone to seed. Every time she laughed, a little fluff blew off the crown and drifted down onto somebody, and wherever it landed, something happened. A boy found a penny. A pie slid off a table into a dog's mouth. A fat merchant sat down on a bench that had not been there a moment before. Nobody could say if these things were good luck or bad luck, and the lady did not seem to care which.

She was Bellasorte, Lady of Luck, of the Summer Court. Her sister in the Winter Court never forgot a broken promise. Bellasorte had never counted anything in her life. She only cared that something happened.

A bit of fluff landed on the weaver's tongue just as he bowed before the king.

"My daughter," said the weaver, "can spin straw into gold."

He had meant well. He had only wanted to compliment his daughter. He had meant to say practically, or almost like, or anything else that would have been reasonable.

The king's eyes went small and shiny, like two coins at the bottom of a well.

That night Mara was locked in a tower room with a spinning wheel and a mountain of straw.

"Spin it into gold by morning," said the king, "or I'll have your head." Then the key turned, and his footsteps went away down the stairs.

Mara's stomach went hot. Not a little warm. Hot like a coal had been dropped in it, and the heat climbed into her chest and her face and her fists. She kicked the straw. It went everywhere. She kicked the wheel, and it hurt her toe, and that made the coal burn hotter. Her father had lied. The king was greedy. And she was the one locked in a tower.

She stood there, breathing hard, with straw in her hair.

Her grandmother, who had buried two husbands and outlived a war, kept three questions for nights like this one. Mara did not want them. She asked them anyway, through her teeth.

Is it wrong, or only unwelcome? It was wrong. Grandmother would have agreed.

Is it yours to mend? Her father's lie was not hers to mend. The king's greed was not hers to mend. But the straw was right there in front of her.

What game are you really playing? Not "make gold." Nobody could win that one. The real game was "be alive in the morning."

So she picked the straw out of her hair, sat down at the wheel, and started to spin. Her hands shook. The thread came out lumpy and yellow and very much straw.

Then there was a knock at the window. Which was strange, because the window was forty feet up.

On the sill stood a little man no taller than a fence post. His beard was tucked into his belt, and from the belt hung a fat ledger-book on a chain, the kind shopkeepers use to write down who owes what. Long ago somebody had cheated him out of something precious, and he had never got over it. Since then he wrote everything down.

"What will you give me," said the little man, "if I spin it for you?"

"My necklace," said Mara.

He licked his pencil and wrote it in the ledger. One necklace. Then he sat at the wheel, and whirr, whirr, whirr, the straw went in and gold came out, all night long, until the room glowed.

The king was delighted, which only made him greedier. The next night it was a bigger room and a bigger pile. The little man came again, and Mara gave him her ring. He wrote it down. One ring. Whirr, whirr, whirr.

The third night the room was so full of straw she had to climb over it to reach the wheel. "Do this," said the king, "and I'll make you my queen."

When the little man came, Mara had nothing left to give.

"Then promise me your first child," he said, "when you are queen."

Her stomach went cold this time, cold and sick. Every bit of her said no. But the sky was already going gray, and the king's boots would be on the stairs with the dawn.

"Yes," she said.

It was a bad bargain. She knew it, and she made it anyway, with the clock running. The little man wrote it down. One child. Whirr, whirr, whirr.

The king married her, because there was a great deal of gold, and gold was the only reason the king did anything. A year went by. Mara had a baby boy with fat cheeks and a loud voice, and she forgot all about the little man.

The little man did not forget. People who keep ledgers never do.

He came in at the nursery window one evening and held out his arms. "A bargain is a bargain," he said. And he was right.

Mara offered him gold. She offered him jewels, horses, half the kingdom. He shook his head at every one. "A promise is worth more than all of that," he said. "If promises don't count, nothing counts."

She cried then. The little man shifted from foot to foot. He was not cruel; he was only very, very fair, and he loved being fair more than he loved anything in the world.

"All right," he said at last. "Here is a fair rule. I will give you three days. If by then you can tell me my name, you keep the child." He wrote the rule in his ledger and underlined it twice.

The first day, Mara guessed every name in every book in the castle. Caspar. Melchior. Balthazar. Bartholomew. "No," said the little man, and wrote each one down.

The second day she guessed strange names. Ribsofbeef. Muttonshanks. Laceleg. "No," said the little man, grinning, and wrote them down too.

The third day she sent her fastest messenger riding into the forest to listen for any name he had never heard before.

By dusk the messenger was lost. The trees all looked the same, the path had split three times, and his horse kept trying to go home.

On a stump by the fork in the road sat a lady in a dress the color of new leaves, flipping a coin. It caught the last of the light every time it spun.

"Heads or tails?" she said.

"I'm sorry, my lady, I'm lost, I—"

"That isn't heads or tails."

"...Heads?"

She caught the coin, looked at it, and laughed so hard that dandelion fluff blew everywhere. "Left, then."

So he went left. And there, in a clearing, around a little fire, a little man with a ledger on his belt was hopping from foot to foot and singing:

Bake today and brew tomorrow,
the queen will have her fill of sorrow,
for none of all her guesses came
to Rumpelstiltskin—that's my name!

That night the little man came in at the nursery window, rubbing his hands. "Well, Your Majesty? Last chance."

"Is it Conrad?" said Mara.

"No!"

"Is it Heinz?"

"No!"

"Is it... Rumpelstiltskin?"

The little man went perfectly still. Then he went pink. Then red. Then a color red doesn't have a name for.

"WHO TOLD YOU?" he shrieked. "Somebody told you! You didn't guess! That's CHEATING!"

And from the windowsill came a laugh, and a little puff of dandelion fluff.

"I did," said Bellasorte, swinging her bare feet. "More or less. I flipped a coin."

"That's not in the RULES!"

"I'm not in the rules," said Bellasorte cheerfully. "I'm luck."

Rumpelstiltskin snatched up his ledger and flipped to the page with the rule underlined twice. If she can tell me my name. It did not say guess. It did not say by herself. By the rule he had written in his own hand, he had lost fair and square.

But his stomach did not care what the ledger said. His stomach said CHEATED, and the coal in it burned so hot that it burned straight through his good sense.

He stamped his foot. He stamped so hard the floorboards cracked, and his leg went straight through the floor up to the knee. He grabbed his other leg to pull himself out, and he pulled with all his fury, harder and harder, and—

RRRRIP.

He split right down the middle, like a wishbone.

The two halves of Rumpelstiltskin stood up, each with one leg, one arm, one eye and half a beard. They looked at each other. "This is YOUR fault," said the left half. "You're the one who stamped," said the right half. "YOU pulled!" And they hopped out the door and down the stairs, still arguing about who had been cheated. As far as anyone knows, they are hopping and arguing still.

Mara held her baby very tight. Her knees were shaking now that it was over. The coin could have come up tails. The messenger could have gone right.

"Why did you help me?" she asked.

Bellasorte tilted her head, and a few more seeds drifted off her crown. "Because he wrote down everything," she said, "except me." And then there was nothing on the windowsill but fluff.

After that, the queen never trusted luck again, not to save her and not to sink her. She learned to spin real flax into real thread, slow and honest, which is a kind of gold that needs no little man to make it. And when her son was old enough to stamp his foot, she sat him on her knee and told him about a little man who was in two places at once.
